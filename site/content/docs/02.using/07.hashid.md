Winsploit knows :stat{k="hashFormats"} hash formats and tells you the matching **`hashcat -m`** mode and **`john --format`** name. It's built in, works offline, and needs no Python.

## In the dashboard

Press `h`, paste a hash, `Enter`. The result appears in the console.

## From the command line

```powershell
winsploit hashid 5f4dcc3b5aa765d61d8327deb882cf99
```

```text
3 candidates, most likely first:
  MD5   hashcat -m 0     john --format=raw-md5
  NTLM  hashcat -m 1000  john --format=nt
  MD4   hashcat -m 900   john --format=raw-md4
```

Several hashes can be passed at once.

## How it decides

It looks at the hash's **prefix** (`$2y$`, `$6$`, `$argon2id$`…), **separators** (`hash:salt`, `user::domain:…`), **length** and **alphabet** (hex, base64, crypt64), and lists the likely types, most likely first.

::callout{type="note"}
Plain hex digests are inherently ambiguous: MD5, NTLM and MD4 all look the same. That's why some hashes come back as several candidates. Context (where you found it) usually decides.
::

## What it knows

Unix crypt formats (bcrypt, md5crypt, sha256crypt, sha512crypt, yescrypt, DES crypt), Argon2, phpass, Kerberos 5 TGS-REP and AS-REP (etype 23), Domain Cached Credentials 1 and 2, NetNTLMv1 and v2, LDAP SSHA, Django, MySQL, salted MD5/SHA variants, CRC32, LM, and the raw digests (MD4, MD5, NTLM, SHA-1/224/256/384/512, SHA-3, Keccak-256, RIPEMD-160, Whirlpool).
